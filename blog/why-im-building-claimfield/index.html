Why I’m Building ClaimField: Making Mining Claim Planning Easier

Keith Ornbaum · 2026-11-15

Mining claim projects involve a lot more than drawing rectangles on a map.

There’s the land research, claim layout, tie points, terrain, staking routes, field maps, and paperwork. Then someone needs to keep track of who is doing the staking, when it happened, and what still needs to be filed.

Change the grid, and much of that information needs to change with it.

My experience with GIS and this workflow is what led me to start building ClaimField. I’ve seen how tedious the process can be, particularly when the information is spread across mapping software, spreadsheets, documents, and separate calendars. Keeping everything consistent can become a job of its own.

I wanted an easier way to manage it. I also wanted to give people who are interested in staking mining claims a practical tool they could use themselves, without needing paid software or someone else’s help for every routine planning task. My goal is to make ClaimField free and approachable.

Bringing the workflow together

ClaimField is being built for individual claim locators, exploration teams, land professionals, GIS users, and the people organizing the fieldwork.

I want someone to be able to open a project, define the area they’re interested in, review the available land information, and start laying out claims. From there, they should be able to look at the terrain, work out the staking routes, assign workers, and prepare their maps and documents.

Those tasks belong together. If you’ve already entered information while building the map, you shouldn’t have to type it again into a schedule or document template.

That’s the part I’ve been spending a lot of time on: making sure the different tools work together as the project changes.

Build the claim grid and keep the details connected

Once you’ve defined your area of interest, you can generate a claim grid and edit it directly on the map.

You can move claims, resize boundaries, adjust their orientation, and manage discovery or site points and tie-point information. As you edit the grid, ClaimField recalculates the related planning geometry and field details.

For me, this is a big part of the app’s value. If I move a boundary, I want the coordinates and supporting information to follow that change. I don’t want to spend the next hour checking several different files to make sure they still agree.

ClaimField also generates draft Notices of Location (NOLs) and Certificates of Location (COLs) using the current project geometry and the information you’ve entered. As you build and revise the grid, the document drafts draw from those project details.

There are facts the app can’t supply for you. Actual location dates, locator information, field observations, monument details, and signatures still need to come from the people doing the work. Missing information is flagged so you can see what remains to be completed.

Start with the land research

Before you start designing a claim grid, you need to research the land.

ClaimField brings together map-based screening, Public Land Survey System information, and other reference layers to help with that work. It supports a research process that includes checking official records through the BLM’s Mineral & Land Records System, or MLRS.

MLRS provides research maps, case information, and tools for managing mineral and land records. ClaimField helps organize the planning work around that research, so you can keep the area you’re reviewing and the grid you’re designing in the same project.

The initial supported state profiles are Nevada, Idaho, Utah, and Arizona. I plan to add more states over time. Each expansion needs a careful look at the available data, documentation, and requirements, so I’m taking that work state by state.

Understand the terrain before sending a crew

A grid can look simple on a map and be a very different job once you’re walking it.

ClaimField uses Terrain3D elevation data to show the terrain within your area of interest. You can review elevation statistics and a colored elevation grid in 3D, from above, or from the side. That gives you another way to assess the area before deciding how to approach the staking work.

The route-planning tools use that terrain information too.

You can set access, start, and finish points, identify the field workers, and generate routes based on the planned staking points, sampled elevation, slope, and travel difficulty. If several people are working on the project, ClaimField can divide the work into separate worker routes.

I want those tools to help with the practical questions: Where should we start? How should we split up the grid? Are we sending someone across a steep section when there may be an easier approach?

The routes are still estimates. Actual access, barriers, ground conditions, and safety need to be checked before a crew relies on them.

Connect the map to the schedule

Once you have a plan, you need to get people into the field.

The Scheduling workspace includes Calendar, Timeline, and List views for managing crews and individual workers. Generated worker routes can create editable planned visits, which saves you from having to rebuild the assignments in a separate calendar.

You can adjust dates, worker assignments, and visit durations, review availability and scheduling conflicts, and export calendar files to use elsewhere.

ClaimField can also prepare field maps with or without routes and export checklists for each worker. After the work is done, reviewed CSV imports can bring actual staking dates, worker names, notes, and coordinates back into the project.

That return trip matters. The information collected in the field needs to make its way back into the records and supporting documents, rather than staying in a separate spreadsheet.

A practical tool, built from a practical problem

I started ClaimField because I know this process can be difficult to organize, and I wanted to do something about it.

I’m working toward one place to handle the planning: land research, claim-grid design, terrain review, routes, scheduling, field records, maps, and document preparation. The aim is to spend less time copying information and more time reviewing the work that actually needs your attention.

There are responsibilities the software won’t take away. Official land-status checks, field requirements, and filing obligations still need to be addressed. ClaimField prepares and organizes information; it does not submit filings to MLRS or certify that a claim is legally valid. BLM and county fees are also separate from my goal of providing a free planning tool.

ClaimField’s public beta is coming soon. I’ll keep sharing updates as I work through testing, refine the tools, and get account access ready.

I’m looking forward to seeing how people use it and hearing where it can make their work easier.
