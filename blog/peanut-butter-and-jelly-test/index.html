The Peanut Butter and Jelly Test: Why AI Still Needs Human Know How

Keith Ornbaum · 2026-11-02

Back in middle school, we were given a familiar challenge: write instructions for an alien from another world to make a peanut butter and jelly sandwich.

It sounded easy. Everyone knows how to make one, right?

But the alien had never been to Earth. It didn’t know what bread was, what peanut butter or jelly looked like, or what a knife was for. It didn’t know where to find the ingredients, how to open the containers, or which side of the bread to spread them on. If we skipped a step because it seemed obvious to us, the alien could get stuck or make a very strange sandwich.

That classroom exercise was really a lesson in communication. To get the result we wanted, we had to break a familiar task into clear, ordered steps and account for what the person following them didn't know.

That lesson feels just as relevant today as it did 20 years ago.

AI can follow instructions, but it needs direction

I’m still learning how to use AI. I’ve used it to build tools, write scripts, develop applications, and troubleshoot errors. The more I work with it, the more I see that getting a useful result depends on more than asking a clever question.

AI needs context. It needs a clear goal, accurate details, and instructions that explain what success should look like. If I leave out an important step or assume that only makes sense to me, the result may be incomplete, incorrect, or headed in the wrong direction.

That is the peanut butter and jelly test: Have I explained the task clearly enough for someone without my background to follow it?

A prompt like “make a sandwich” leaves too much unsaid. A better set of instructions explains what ingredients and tools are needed, what to do with them, and how to tell when the sandwich is finished. The same principle applies when asking AI to help write a script or build an application. It helps to explain the purpose, the environment, the expected behavior, and the constraints, not just the features you have in mind.

Experience helps you recognize what the instructions leave out

Clear instructions matter, but so does the knowledge of the person give them.

Years of working in an industry teach you things that are hard to capture in a short prompt. You learn how the pieces fit together, what tends to go wrong, which details matter, and what to check before making the work complete. You build judgment through practice: what to do, what not to do, and how to approach the task in the first place.

In GIS, for example, a map can look polished and still be misleading if the underlying data, coordinate systems, or projections are wrong. Knowing how to use the software is only part of the work. You also need to understand the information you’re working with and the decisions the map is meant to support.

That kind of background changes how you work with AI. You can give it more useful direction, spot when an answer sounds plausible but misses something important and ask better follow-up questions. You can test the result against real needs instead of accepting it simply because it looks finished.

AI can help me move faster, explore an idea, or get unstuck. My experience helps me decide what to ask, what to verify, and whether the result is useful.

Building with AI is still hands-on work

Using AI doesn’t mean handing over the whole job and hoping for the best. In my experience, it works more like a collaboration: I bring the goal, the background, and the judgment; AI can help turn that into drafts, code, troubleshooting steps, or new possibilities. Then I review what it produced, try it, find what needs work, and refine the instructions.

That process takes patience. Sometimes the first attempt works. Sometimes it reveals that I haven’t explained the problem well enough, or that I need to learn more before I can judge the answer. Either way, each round teaches me something about the tool and the work itself.

The same is true of any idea I want to pursue. Knowing what I’m trying to accomplish, understanding the parts involved, and being able to describe the steps gives me a better chance of turning an idea into something that works.

The goal is to make useful things

I’m still learning, and I’m still building tools that help me personally. I hope some of them can help other people, too. I couldn’t use AI effectively to build, support, or improve these projects without the knowledge and skills I’ve developed in my field and the experiences I have had.

Technology may be new, but the fundamentals aren’t. Understand the task. Know the materials. Explain the steps. Check the result. Learn from what happens next.

That’s how you make a peanut butter and jelly sandwich for an alien, and, in its own way, how you use AI to build something worthwhile.
