What Came In, What Went Out, and What’s Next?

See how ClearWorthPlanner brings actual transactions, planned income and expenses, and future projections together to help you make better decisions with your money.

Keith OrnbaumNov 22, 20264 min read

In this article

  • Actual shows what happened
  • Planned shows what you expect
  • Projected shows what could happen next
  • What you learn by seeing all three

When my wife and I sit down every two weeks to talk about money, we look at what came in, what went out, and what’s coming up. Sometimes the conversation is as simple as checking which bills are due before the next paycheck. Other times, we’re trying to figure out whether a trip or a bigger expense fits into the next few months.

Those conversations helped shape how I built ClearWorthPlanner. I wanted to see what actually happened, what we expected to happen, and what might happen if we changed the plan. In the app, those three views are Actual, Planned, and Projected.

Actual shows what happened

Actual records are the money that has already moved. A paycheck landed in your checking account. The electric bill cleared. You bought groceries. You can bring that activity into ClearWorthPlanner from a bank file or use Secure Sync to connect a supported institution through Plaid. Bank linking is optional, so you can choose the method that works for you.

With Secure Sync, you verify your email, connect through Plaid, select the accounts you want to use, and review the activity before importing it. That review matters. You can correct a suggested category, confirm whether a transaction is income or an expense, and check which account it belongs to. You can also add transactions manually and review posted records under Transactions & Expense Tracking.

The benefit of Actual is that it gives you something concrete to work from. If groceries routinely come in above the amount you budgeted, you have a reason to look at that number again. You might change what you spend, or you might decide the original budget needs to be more realistic.

Planned shows what you expect

Planned records are the income and expenses you expect on a schedule. Think of a paycheck every other Friday, a mortgage payment on the first, or a monthly insurance bill. You can also plan for expenses that vary, like groceries or electricity, by entering the amount you expect to spend.

In ClearWorthPlanner’s Budget view, you can review your plan by week, pay period, or month. You can assign paycheck income to specific expenses and review which payments are coming up. This is where timing starts to matter. A month can look fine on paper, yet several bills can come due before the paycheck you intended to use for them.

The plan also gives your actual transactions context. If the electric bill came in $40 higher than expected, seeing both numbers tells you where the difference came from. Over a few months, those comparisons can help you decide which estimates to adjust and which changes are worth a closer look.

Projected shows what could happen next

Projected income and expenses are the assumptions you use to explore a future decision. What if your insurance goes up? What if you take on extra work for a few months? What if you want to save for a vacation while keeping up with your regular bills?

That’s what Planning Studio is for. You can use your regular plan as a starting point, look at recent actual activity to see whether your estimates are realistic, then adjust the future income, expenses, and savings in a scenario. You can save different scenarios and come back to compare them. Exploring a higher insurance bill or a lower paycheck leaves your actual transaction history and recurring plan intact.

I find this especially helpful when the answer depends on a few things going right. You can see how a decision looks under your original assumptions and how much room you have if the numbers come in differently.

What you learn by seeing all three

Say you planned for $4,200 in income and $3,600 in expenses last month. That left $600 on paper. Your actual income was $4,100, and actual expenses were $3,800, so the month ended with $300 left.

Now suppose you want to set aside $200 a month for a trip, and you expect another $150 a month in expenses. Starting from the planned $600, that leaves $250. If last month’s actual results repeat, those same commitments would put you $50 short.

That’s where the visuals help. In the dashboard’s Compare view, you can see planned and actual cash flow side by side and trace where the difference came from. Maybe one expense was unusually high, or maybe several categories ran over a little. In Planning Studio, you can then see how those differences affect the months ahead and try a different savings amount or timeline.

When my wife and I sit down for our next check-in, those are the kinds of conversations I want the numbers to help us have. What changed? Was it a one-time thing? Does it change what we want to do next? Seeing the actual records, the plan, and the projection gives us a shared place to start.
